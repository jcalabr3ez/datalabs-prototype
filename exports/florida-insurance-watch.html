Florida Insurance Watch

What Florida households pay for homeowners insurance, what their coverage now includes, and how both have changed. Compiled from state filings.

Premiums remain high even as the market stabilizes. Both facts are accurate. Affordability was the dominant theme in a July 2026 Tampa focus group, where participants described their premiums as "just as burdensome as mortgage payments." The market did stabilize and average rates have begun to fall. However, the relief arrived late and is small against several years of increases, and measured per dollar of protection the statewide price has flattened rather than fallen. This page sets out what changed, drawn from state filings, and what it means for a household.

Focus group: Pioneer Institute, Tampa, July 2, 2026.

How can premiums stay high if the market is improving?

Both are accurate, and they are consistent.

The reforms addressed what they were designed to address: the market stopped contracting, insurers re-entered, and the state moved away from a near-term solvency crisis. That progress is the reason rate decreases have replaced double-digit increases.

Rate relief nonetheless arrived late and modest relative to the preceding increases. Between 2019 and 2022 the average Florida premium rose from about $1,988 to $2,677, while the national average rose more slowly, from $1,272 to $1,569. An 8.8 percent reduction in 2026 does not offset several years of increases, so premiums are below the prior year yet remain well above earlier levels.

A second factor complicates the averages. Statewide, measured per dollar of coverage across every admitted insurer, the price of protection has been roughly flat since late 2023, so the premium pause is real rather than an artifact of people buying less coverage. On Citizens’ own book the same measure rose about 18 percent, but Citizens shrank roughly 80 percent over that period as private carriers selected which policies to remove, so its rising unit price says more about the harder risks left behind than about market-wide prices.

Sources: premium run-up, NAIC average HO-3 premium by state, 2019 and 2022, via the Insurance Information Institute; price per $1,000 of coverage, Pioneer calculation from Citizens premium and exposure filings.

Is the price of protection actually falling?

Two charts answer it. The first shows the bill: the average annual premium across every admitted insurer in Florida. The second shows the sticker price of protection itself: what one year of coverage costs for each $100,000 of home value insured. The bill went up and then stopped; the sticker price barely moved. The dashed line is Citizens, the state insurer, whose remaining book is the expensive exception.

Read the two panels together. The average premium (1a) climbed from $2,798 in mid-2022 to a $3,748 peak in late 2025, then flattened; the net rise through early 2026 is 32.6 percent. But the price of protection itself (1b) barely moved: a year of coverage for each $100,000 of home value cost $459 statewide in mid-2022 and $485 in early 2026, and has been essentially flat since late 2023. Premiums rose mostly because the amount of coverage per home rose about 25 percent, which largely reflects the higher cost of rebuilding, not households buying more protection. The exception is Citizens, the state insurer: on its shrinking book, the same $100,000 of coverage went from $782 to $927 a year, up 18.5 percent, and now costs nearly twice the market rate. That gap is best read as composition, not market prices: Citizens lost roughly four-fifths of its policies as private carriers selected which ones to take, so the harder, costlier homes are what remain.

▶Why some questions cannot be answered yet

Florida law requires both halves of this picture. Every residential insurer must file detailed market data, and OIR must publish the statewide aggregate: under s. 624.424(10)(b), Florida Statutes, the office “shall aggregate on a statewide basis the data submitted by each insurer or insurer group under paragraph (a) and make such data publicly available by publishing such data on the office’s website within 1 month after each quarterly and annual filing,” and such information, “when aggregated on a statewide basis as to an individual insurer or insurer group, is not a trade secret” and “is not subject to the public records exemption for trade secrets.” So market-wide totals, exposure, and premium are public, and this page now uses them.

What insurers can shield is the geographic detail. Under s. 624.4213, Florida Statutes, a filer may designate its submission a trade secret, and OIR excludes those companies from its public company-and-county query tool. OIR’s own site lists 86 companies excluded on that basis, beginning with State Farm Florida in 2014 and expanding sharply from late 2022. The homeowners policies still visible company by company in that tool fell from about 1.29 million at the end of 2022 to about 405,000 at the end of 2025, not because the market shrank, but because the insurers writing it went dark in that tool. Their statewide totals remain published; their county and zip-code detail does not. As reform moved policies off the state insurer, whose book is fully public at every level, they largely landed with trade secret filers.

The practical consequence: this page can tell you what the whole market pays, how big it is, and which carriers are growing or retreating statewide, all of which remain published insurer by insurer. What cannot be answered from public data is anything beneath the statewide line for most of the market: who writes coverage in a given county, where takeout policies actually landed, and how coverage terms compare, since deductibles and limits are published for no insurer at all. Citizens is the only insurer whose book is public at every level: monthly, by county, and with coverage detail.

Sources: s. 624.424(10)(b), Florida Statutes (2025) (aggregation and publication mandate); s. 624.4213, Florida Statutes (2025) (trade secret designation); FL OIR, Residential Market Share Reports (Trade Secret Companies list and statewide files); FL OIR Property Market Intelligence Report (Totals Report), reporting periods ending December 31, 2022 and December 31, 2025, generated through the OIR Market Intelligence interface, for the visible-policies figures. Calculation: Pioneer Institute.

Why does a smaller state insurer help you?

Citizens became Florida's largest home insurer during the crisis. Because of its surcharge authority, a large Citizens represents a risk to every policyholder. It is now smaller than at any point since before the pandemic. Source: Citizens, Policies in Force.

Does a homeowners policy cover flooding? No, and it matters

Nearly half of Citizens' 2024 hurricane claims closed without payment, largely because the damage was flood.

Of the more than 76,000 Citizens claims filed after hurricanes Debby, Helene, and Milton as of the January 2025 testimony, 44 percent had closed without payment and 11 percent remained open. For Debby and Helene, only 24 and 28 percent of claims were paid, because most of the damage was flooding, which a standard homeowners policy excludes in every state. Citizens paid about $823 million on the season's claims overall.

Flood coverage requires a separate policy, through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private flood insurer, and typically carries a waiting period before it takes effect. A homeowner who drops wind coverage to cut cost and carries no flood policy retains very little hurricane protection at all.

Source: testimony of Citizens President/CEO Tim Cerio to the Florida Senate Banking and Insurance Committee, January 14, 2025, as reported by the Florida Phoenix. Claim counts and payment rates are Citizens figures presented in that testimony. The filed-claim total is as reported at the January 14, 2025 hearing and was still rising at that date; Citizens also reported 34,645 claims closed without payment, which at the stated 44 percent share implies a season total near 78,700 (34,645 / 0.44), a derived figure.

The reforms worked on the risk the state was carrying. They have not yet been shown to work on affordability, and the state does not measure it. Taxpayer catastrophe exposure is down 87 percent and the projected post-storm assessment is zero below a 1-in-360-year event. The market is profitable again, litigation has fallen sharply, and capital is returning. Three findings below qualify that conclusion: headline takeout numbers overstate progress until new policies entering are netted out; on Citizens' own shrinking book the price of coverage per dollar of protection rose even as average premiums fell, reflecting the harder risks left behind rather than market prices; and Florida publishes no coverage-adjusted affordability figure of its own. Measured from the state's aggregate files across every admitted insurer, the statewide price of protection has been flat since late 2023: a pause, not yet a decline. This view lays out what changed, what it cost, and what is still unmeasured.

Capital is entering, not merely returning.

Since the reforms, 20 new residential writers have been approved, bringing more than $850 million of capital; more telling than the count is where competition reappeared. Writers of wind-only condominium association coverage in Broward, Miami-Dade, and Palm Beach counties, the hardest niche in the state and the kind of risk Citizens exists to absorb, have grown from one to five. And the capital markets have repriced Florida risk directly: Citizens' 2026 catastrophe bond, Everglades Re II 2026-1, was upsized by a third to $600 million and priced below guidance, a softening signal that cuts against the record-high reinsurance price share in Figure 7 if it holds.

Sources: FL OIR, May 20, 2026 (entrant count, capital, and condominium writers); Artemis, May 13, 2026 (catastrophe bond pricing).

Do gross takeout figures overstate progress?

The takeout number usually cited is the gross count: policies private carriers took from Citizens. It ignores the policies flowing in behind them, so a heavy takeout year can still end with Citizens larger than it started. Read the table left to right: what was taken out, what the book actually did, and the inflow that explains the difference.

A further caution: any single quarter misleads

Assumption rounds pause during the heart of hurricane season and resume in the fall. The result is a predictable pattern: the book grows or flattens each summer and declines sharply each autumn. The autumn declines are also deepening.

Has the probability of loss fallen since the reforms?

Exposure and policy counts measure size. The probability question is different: how likely is a loss of a given scale, and how likely is the loss that reaches the public? The exhibit tracks the modeled loss with a 1 percent annual probability, the 100-year probable maximum loss, across each published vintage of Citizens' book.
Figure 5 The 1-percent-annual-probability loss: Citizens' 100-year PML by book vintage
Reading left to right: the 2018 book carried a $5.3 billion 100-year loss; the crisis-era book roughly tripled it to about $18.0 billion by September 2023; depopulation then walked it down to $6.5 billion on the modeled 2026 book, and Pioneer's scaling to the smaller actual June 2026 book puts it at roughly $4.0 to $5.1 billion (gold bar, estimate). The probability of the loss that reaches the public moved the same way: Citizens' 2019 statutory report showed an $851 million assessable shortfall at the 1-in-250 event on the 2018 book, while the 2026 program projects no surcharge or assessment below a 1-in-360 event. One finding cuts the other way, and it is the honest counterpoint: measured per dollar of insured value, the remaining book is riskier, not safer. The 100-year loss was about 2.9 percent of insured value on the September 2023 book and about 5.0 to 6.4 percent on the 2026 book, because depopulation removed the easier risks and left the catastrophe-intense core.

Whose risk is it? Citizens' slice of the state's modeled catastrophe

Citizens is now a small insurer. Its share of the state's hurricane risk is not as small. The chart compares the two.
Figure 6 Citizens' share of Florida's modeled 1-in-100 residential hurricane loss
The two bars describe today's book. Citizens insures about 2.5 percent of Florida's residential property value, but it carries roughly 5 to 7 percent of the state's modeled 1-in-100 hurricane loss (gold, estimate): between two and three times its market weight. That is adverse selection made visible: the policies private carriers chose not to take carry more than their share of the next catastrophe, which is the case for aiming mitigation money at the remaining Citizens book first. At the September 2023 peak, the same comparison was close to proportionate: Citizens then held roughly 26 percent of the modeled statewide loss, but it was also the state's largest insurer, so shrinking the company concentrated its risk rather than spreading it.

What risk does Citizens still hold?

Citizens' financing question has inverted. The catastrophe tail is now almost entirely transferred to the Cat Fund and private reinsurance; what the corporation retains is the ordinary bad year. The exhibit tracks the transfer program itself: how much protection Citizens bought each year, and what it cost.
Figure 7 Nine years of risk transfer: coverage purchased and its price, 2017 through 2025
Bars show catastrophe reinsurance coverage purchased each year, Cat Fund plus private and capital markets, from the audited statements' reinsurance notes; the line shows what it cost, catastrophe ceded premiums as a share of direct written premium. The time dimension corrects the impression a single-year snapshot gives. Before the reforms, the program barely moved, between $3.2 and $3.7 billion a year from 2017 through 2020, while the book was about to triple: measured against the modeled 100-year loss, coverage actually fell from 0.68 times that loss on the 2018 book to 0.60 times at the 2023 peak, so the state's transfer of tail risk was thinnest exactly when the book was largest. The program then peaked at $10.8 billion in 2023 and has settled near $7.8 billion on a book whose modeled 100-year loss is $6.5 billion, roughly 1.2 times the 1-in-100 event, which is why the projected surcharge threshold now sits beyond a 1-in-360 year. What the program does not remove: per Citizens' modeled severity curve, the uncovered risk lives in the frequency layers, not the tail: a 2024-style three-storm season, scaled to this book's exposure, is roughly a $570 million net year by Pioneer's derivation. The price line is the caution: catastrophe reinsurance now absorbs 28.7 percent of every written premium dollar, the highest share of the nine years, so Citizens' net results are geared to the reinsurance cycle even in quiet seasons.

Constituent sentiment

A July 2026 Tampa focus group found affordability dominating every discussion, with insurance described as "just as burdensome as mortgage payments." Participants were broadly frustrated and skeptical of positive top-line messaging, while giving state leadership a passing grade overall.

"Working just to afford basic living means you can't actually enjoy living."

The systemic figures above and that frustration are not in conflict. The state balance sheet improved materially. Household costs improved late, modestly, and unevenly, and part of the apparent improvement reflects coverage reduction rather than price relief.

Source: Pioneer Institute focus group, Tampa, July 2, 2026. Quotations are from focus group participants.

The Legislature stated its objectives when it passed the reforms. This page grades each one against the record. Objectives are taken from the laws' own official record: the Florida Senate's bill summaries for the 2022 special sessions and the enacting statements for the 2023 tort bill. The evidence is drawn from the same state filings used throughout this tool, and every figure carries its source. The grades themselves are Pioneer Institute judgments; the reader is free to weigh the same evidence differently.

The three reform laws

Ch. 2022-268 (SB 2-D, May 2022) responded to what the Senate's summary called "the increasing lack of access and affordability of property insurance", creating the $2 billion Reinsurance to Assist Policyholders layer and home hardening grants. Ch. 2022-271 (SB 2-A, December 2022) was, in the Senate summary's words, intended to ensure "access to quality, affordable private market property insurance"; it eliminated one-way attorney fees and assignment of benefits, tightened claim payment deadlines, created the optional FORA reinsurance program, and redirected Citizens toward residual status. Ch. 2023-15 (HB 837, March 2023) extended the litigation changes to all lines, with the stated purpose to "decrease frivolous lawsuits and prevent predatory practices of trial attorneys".

Sources: Ch. 2022-268 (SB 2-D), Ch. 2022-271 (SB 2-A), Ch. 2023-15 (HB 837), Laws of Florida; Florida Senate bill summaries, 2022D and 2022A; Executive Office of the Governor, March 24, 2023.
Scope and completeness. Three boundaries apply to this record. HB 837's stated objectives extend well beyond property insurance, including modified comparative negligence, a two-year statute of limitations for general negligence, uniform medical-damages standards, and expanded property-owner immunity; this page grades only its property insurance litigation effects. SB 7052 (Ch. 2023-172, June 2023), the Insurer Accountability Act, is a fourth reform-era property insurance law addressing claims-handling conduct and OIR enforcement; it is outside the laws graded here, and its subject matter bears on objectives 6 and 8. SB 4-D (Ch. 2022-269, May 2022), the building-safety act passed in the same special session, revised the Building Code's roof-replacement rule and created the condominium milestone-inspection regime; it is adjacent to this record and not graded here.

Report card

Nine objectives drawn from the legislative record, graded A through F, with Incomplete where the mandate is still phasing in or public outcome data does not yet exist. Grades reflect data through June 30, 2026.

In brief: the market-structure objectives earned the strongest marks, affordability the weakest, transparency is mixed, and three mandates cannot yet be fully judged.

Objective, as statedGradeWhat the record shows
1. Restore access to private market coverage SB 2-A, in the Senate summary's words, was intended to ensure “access to quality, affordable private market property insurance.” Fla. S. Comm. on Banking & Ins., SB 2-A Summary (2022A) B+
Twenty new residential property insurers approved since the reforms, bringing more than $850 million in new capital through May 2026. Writers of wind-only condominium association coverage in Broward, Miami-Dade, and Palm Beach counties grew from one to five. Private carriers assumed 477,821 Citizens policies in 2024, and the takeout trajectory continued through 2025 and the first half of 2026 (see the depopulation exhibits on the policymaker page). The grade stops at B+ for one reason: none of this capacity has met a major hurricane. Until it does, its durability is a promise rather than a record, and Florida has seen promising capacity fail before.
Sources: FL OIR, May 20, 2026 (entrant count, capital, condominium writers); FL OIR Stability Report, Jan 1, 2026 (assumption counts)
2. End excessive litigation SB 2-A eliminated one-way attorney fees and assignment of benefits; HB 837's stated purpose was to "decrease frivolous lawsuits and prevent predatory practices of trial attorneys." Exec. Office of the Governor, Mar. 24, 2023 A−
On Citizens' book, the share of non-catastrophe personal residential claims that end in litigation fell from 10.3 percent (accident year 2023) to 6.6 percent (2024) to 5.0 percent (2025), against 15 to 19 percent in the pre-reform years; litigated claim severity fell from $32,544 to $18,318 over the same accident years. Statewide, Florida's share of the nation's homeowners suits fell from 76 percent (2021) to a preliminary 41.3 percent (2025) while its share of claims fell to 4.9 percent. What keeps the grade short of an A is arithmetic: 41.3 percent of the nation's suits against 4.9 percent of its claims still leaves Florida litigating at roughly eight times the national propensity.
Sources: Citizens, President's Report to the Board of Governors, Jun 23, 2026 (litigation frequency and severity by accident year); FL OIR Stability Report, Jul 1, 2026 (NAIC MCAS shares; 2025 preliminary, final expected Q3 2026)
3. Return Citizens to its residual role SB 2-A required Citizens' rates to be noncompetitive with the admitted market and raised the private-offer eligibility threshold to 20 percent. Fla. S. Comm. on Banking & Ins., SB 2-A Summary (2022A) A
Citizens' policy count fell 80.2 percent from the September 2023 month-end peak of 1,407,805 to 278,246 in June 2026, and its insured exposure fell 87.1 percent, from $618.1 billion to $79.6 billion. Its share of the admitted market is 1.93 percent. For the 2026 season Citizens reports $9.6 billion of claims-paying ability and no policyholder surcharge or emergency assessment until a hurricane worse than 1-in-360 years. Two qualifications temper the A. The first measurable decline arrived 22 months after SB 2-A passed, a lag worth remembering when judging any future reform. And the book left behind costs 18.5 percent more per dollar of protection than in 2023, which is what adverse selection looks like when private carriers choose which policies to take. SB 2-A also directed the consolidation of Citizens’ three accounts into a single account upon retirement of outstanding financing obligations, restructuring the policyholder surcharge from 15 percent per account (a 45 percent maximum) to 15 percent on the single account, the structure behind the assessment figures used on this page.
Sources: Citizens, Policies in Force and exposure filings; Citizens, Jun 23, 2026 (claims-paying ability, assessment threshold); FL OIR Stability Report, Jul 1, 2026 (market share). Price per $1,000 of insured value: Pioneer Institute calculation from Citizens filings
4. Affordable coverage for households SB 2-D addressed "the increasing lack of access and affordability of property insurance"; SB 2-A promised "access to quality, affordable private market property insurance." Fla. S. Bill Summary, SB 2-D (2022D); SB 2-A (2022A) C
The statewide average premium rose from $2,798 in mid-2022 to a $3,748 peak in late 2025, then flattened; the net rise through early 2026 is 32.6 percent. Measured per dollar of protection, the statewide price has been roughly flat since late 2023. Relief began only in 2026: Citizens' approved 8.8 percent statewide multiperil decrease is its first broad cut since 2015, average premiums fell in 51 of 67 counties between the September 2025 and March 2026 readings, and 44 insurers have requested decreases. The C records a plain fact: prices stopped rising, but they stopped at the top. One 8.8 percent cut, however welcome, does not unwind a 32.6 percent climb.
Sources: Pioneer Institute calculation from FL OIR Statewide Summary by Company and Policy Type (premium and coverage series); Citizens, Mar 4, 2026 (rate decrease); FL OIR Stability Report, Jul 1, 2026 (county changes, decrease requests)
5. Bridge the reinsurance shortage SB 2-D created the $2 billion RAP layer below the Cat Fund; SB 2-A added the optional $1 billion FORA program for 2023, both as temporary state backstops. Fla. S. Bill Summary, SB 2-D (2022D); SB 2-A (2022A) B
The objective was to bridge until private capacity returned, and private capacity has returned on better terms: Citizens reports its 2026 reinsurance rates fell nearly 30 percent from the prior year, and its Everglades Re II 2026-1 catastrophe bond was upsized by a third to $600 million and priced below guidance. A full grade waits on an accounting the state has never published: what RAP and FORA actually paid out, and what the bridge cost. The destination was reached; whether the bridge was worth its price is not knowable from public data.
Sources: Citizens, Jun 23, 2026 (reinsurance pricing); Artemis, May 13, 2026 (catastrophe bond terms)
6. Prompt, fair claims handling SB 2-A cut the deadline to pay or deny a claim from 90 to 60 days and to acknowledge a claim from 14 to 7 days. Fla. S. Comm. on Banking & Ins., SB 2-A Summary (2022A) Incomplete
The deadlines are law, but no comprehensive public dataset measures compliance or claim outcomes across insurers, so the objective cannot yet be graded. The one large test to date is ambiguous: 44 percent of the more than 76,000 Citizens claims from the 2024 hurricanes closed without payment, largely because the damage was flood, which a standard homeowners policy excludes in every state. That is a coverage-design result, not a speed result, and it is measured for only one insurer.
Source: testimony of Citizens President/CEO Tim Cerio to the Florida Senate Banking and Insurance Committee, January 14, 2025, as reported by the Florida Phoenix
7. Close the flood protection gap SB 2-A requires Citizens personal lines policyholders to secure and maintain flood insurance, phased by home value through 2027. Fla. S. Comm. on Banking & Ins., SB 2-A Summary (2022A) Incomplete
The mandate the 2024 season proved necessary is still phasing in, and applies only to Citizens policyholders, a book now covering under 2 percent of the market. For hurricanes Debby and Helene, only 24 and 28 percent of Citizens claims were paid because most damage was flooding. No public series yet measures flood take-up across the market, so the objective cannot be graded before the phase-in completes in 2027.
Sources: Fla. S. Bill Summary, SB 2-A (2022A) (mandate and timetable); Cerio testimony, January 14, 2025, as reported by the Florida Phoenix (payment rates)
8. Strengthen regulatory oversight and market transparency SB 2-A, per the Senate summary, "strengthens the regulatory authority of the Office of Insurance Regulation over property insurers"; SB 2-D created the Property Insurer Stability Unit, with duties including enhanced monitoring, annual catastrophe stress tests, insolvency reviews, and semiannual market reports, and mandated statewide data publication. Fla. S. Comm. on Banking & Ins., SB 2-A Summary (2022A); SB 2-D Summary (2022D) B−
The machinery was built and much of it demonstrably runs. The Stability Unit's semiannual reports have published on schedule, and the January 1 and July 1, 2026 editions supply figures throughout this tool. The statewide data-publication mandate works: every market-wide series on this page was recomputed from the files it requires, and s. 624.424(10)(b) makes that data expressly not a trade secret. Two gaps hold the grade down. County-level transparency moved the opposite direction: 86 insurers have designated their county detail a trade secret, and the policies visible in OIR's county tool fell from about 1.29 million to about 405,000 (see "Why some questions cannot be answered yet" on the homeowner page). And no published output from the mandated annual catastrophe stress tests or the required insolvency reviews could be located for this tool, so those duties cannot be confirmed from public records.
Sources: Fla. S. Bill Summary, SB 2-D (2022D) (Stability Unit duties, publication mandate); SB 2-A (2022A) (regulatory authority); s. 624.424(10)(b), Florida Statutes; FL OIR, Residential Market Share Reports (Trade Secret Companies list); FL OIR Stability Reports (publication cadence)
9. Reduce hurricane losses through home mitigation SB 2-D appropriated $150 million to the My Safe Florida Home program for inspections and matching grants, "thereby reducing their vulnerability to hurricane damage and helping decrease the cost of residential property insurance." Fla. S. Bill Summary, SB 2-D (2022D) Incomplete
The program was funded, with $25 million allocated to hurricane mitigation inspections and $115 million to grants, and the law built in its own outcome metric: the Department of Financial Services must report annually the insurance premium discounts the mitigation generates. That discount series has not been verified for this tool, and no public dataset yet links completed grants to realized premium reductions or to takeout eligibility, so the objective cannot be graded on outcomes. The stakes are documented elsewhere on this page: the remaining Citizens book carries an estimated two to three times its market weight in modeled tail risk, which is where each mitigation dollar buys the most.
Source: Fla. S. Bill Summary, SB 2-D (2022D) (appropriation, allocations, purpose language, and the annual discount-reporting requirement)
How to read these grades. Three cautions apply to every row. First, association is not causation: the 2025 season brought minimal storm activity to Florida, and some unknowable share of the improvement is weather. Second, grades on the state's balance sheet (rows 1 through 3) are provisional until a major landfall stresses the new capacity, the smaller Citizens, and the tightened claim deadlines at the same time; that event, not a quiet season, is the real examination. Third, the affordability grade measures outcomes against the laws' own stated promise, not against what was politically achievable; readers who weight the counterfactual differently may reasonably grade higher. About this tool. Florida Insurance Watch is a Pioneer Institute DataLabs research tool, published in beta and versioned. Corrections and data refreshes will be logged in a public changelog beginning at launch, and the version and data vintage should be cited alongside any figure taken from this page. It is a living data tool, not a static report.

Data sources, accuracy, and revisions. Figures are compiled in good faith from public filings issued by third parties, principally Citizens Property Insurance Corporation and the Florida Office of Insurance Regulation, and are accurate to the stated vintage at the time of verification. Those bodies publish on their own schedules and may revise, restate, or withdraw prior figures without notice; all figures here are point-in-time and may not match a source that has since been updated. The same measure is sometimes reported on different bases or as of different dates: for example, gross assumptions are stated as of June 16, 2026 against policies in force as of June 30, and the combined ratio is the weighted average OIR now uses, where its earlier reports used a straight average. This tool and its contents are provided as is, without warranty of any kind, express or implied, including accuracy, completeness, or fitness for a particular purpose.
Derived figures and model outputs. Measures labeled as Pioneer Institute calculations, including depopulation efficiency, implied inflow, price per $1,000 of insured value, and the market-wide premium and price-of-protection series, are derived from the cited filings, are shown with their arithmetic, and have not been reviewed or endorsed by the issuing agencies. Forward-looking figures are model outputs, not guarantees: in particular, post-storm outcomes are not predicted here, and the no-surcharge projection is a modeled annual figure that a sufficiently severe season would overturn.
Known limits of what this page can show. Figures are statewide and county averages, not quotes: an individual premium depends on the home, county, coverage, and insurer. A lower average premium can reflect reduced coverage rather than a lower price, so deductibles and wind coverage should be read alongside any premium figure. Causation is not established: the reform period coincided with a softening reinsurance market, a Cat Fund rate reduction, and quiet storm seasons, and separating reform from cycle requires a counterfactual this page does not attempt. Premium in force is a volume measure, not a price. Non-renewals, exits from the insured market, and the uninsured share are not confirmed against primary sources and are excluded from analysis.
