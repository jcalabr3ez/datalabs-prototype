Florida Insurance Watch

What Florida households pay for homeowners insurance, what their coverage now includes, and how both have changed. Compiled from state filings.

Premiums remain high even as the market stabilizes. Both facts are accurate. Affordability was the dominant theme in a July 2026 Tampa focus group, where participants described their premiums as "just as burdensome as mortgage payments." The market did stabilize and average rates have begun to fall. Florida nonetheless remains the most expensive state in the country for homeowners insurance, the relief arrived late and is small against several years of increases, and measured per dollar of protection the statewide price has flattened rather than fallen. This page sets out what changed, drawn from state filings, and what it means for a household.

Most-expensive-state ranking: Insurify, Insuring the American Homeowner Report (2026), 2025 statewide average $8,292, first among states; rankings vary with methodology, and Florida places first in the major cost surveys. Focus group: Pioneer Institute, Tampa, July 2, 2026.

How can premiums stay high if the market is improving?

Both are accurate, and they are consistent.

The reforms addressed what they were designed to address: the market stopped contracting, insurers re-entered, and the state moved away from a near-term solvency crisis. That progress is the reason rate decreases have replaced double-digit increases.

Rate relief nonetheless arrived late and modest relative to the preceding increases. Between 2019 and 2022 the average Florida premium rose from about $1,988 to $2,677, while the national average rose more slowly, from $1,272 to $1,569. An 8.8 percent reduction in 2026 does not offset several years of increases, so premiums are below the prior year yet remain well above earlier levels.

A second factor complicates the averages. Statewide, measured per dollar of coverage across every admitted insurer, the price of protection has been roughly flat since late 2023, so the premium pause is real rather than an artifact of people buying less coverage. On Citizens’ own book the same measure rose about 18 percent, but Citizens shrank roughly 80 percent over that period as private carriers selected which policies to remove, so its rising unit price says more about the harder risks left behind than about market-wide prices.

Sources: premium run-up, NAIC average HO-3 premium by state, 2019 and 2022, via the Insurance Information Institute; price per $1,000 of coverage, Pioneer calculation from Citizens premium and exposure filings.

Is the price of protection actually falling?

Two charts answer it. The first shows the bill: the average annual premium across every admitted insurer in Florida. The second shows the sticker price of protection itself: what one year of coverage costs for each $100,000 of home value insured. The bill went up and then stopped; the sticker price barely moved. The dashed line is Citizens, the state insurer, whose remaining book is the expensive exception.
Scope and source, before you read the chart: the two statewide series come from OIR’s quarterly Statewide Summary by Company and Policy Type, sixteen files, Q2 2022 through Q1 2026, summed over owner-occupied homeowners policies excluding wind-only; the Citizens line comes from Citizens’ own premium and exposure filings, which run monthly and one quarter further. Calculation: Pioneer Institute; every figure was recomputed through two independent parses and matches the statewide averages OIR has cited publicly. The fold beneath the chart explains what is public for the rest of the market and what is not.

Read the two panels together. The average premium (1a) climbed from $2,798 in mid-2022 to a $3,748 peak in late 2025, then flattened; the net rise through early 2026 is 32.6 percent. But the price of protection itself (1b) barely moved: a year of coverage for each $100,000 of home value cost $459 statewide in mid-2022 and $485 in early 2026, and has been essentially flat since late 2023. Premiums rose mostly because the amount of coverage per home rose about 25 percent, which largely reflects the higher cost of rebuilding, not households buying more protection. The exception is Citizens, the state insurer: on its shrinking book, the same $100,000 of coverage went from $782 to $927 a year, up 18.5 percent, and now costs nearly twice the market rate. That gap is best read as composition, not market prices: Citizens lost roughly four-fifths of its policies as private carriers selected which ones to take, so the harder, costlier homes are what remain.

▶Why some questions cannot be answered yet

Florida law requires both halves of this picture. Every residential insurer must file detailed market data, and OIR must publish the statewide aggregate: under s. 624.424(10)(b), Florida Statutes, the office “shall aggregate on a statewide basis the data submitted by each insurer or insurer group under paragraph (a) and make such data publicly available by publishing such data on the office’s website within 1 month after each quarterly and annual filing,” and such information, “when aggregated on a statewide basis as to an individual insurer or insurer group, is not a trade secret” and “is not subject to the public records exemption for trade secrets.” So market-wide totals, exposure, and premium are public, and this page now uses them.

What insurers can shield is the geographic detail. Under s. 624.4213, Florida Statutes, a filer may designate its submission a trade secret, and OIR excludes those companies from its public company-and-county query tool. OIR’s own site lists 86 companies excluded on that basis, beginning with State Farm Florida in 2014 and expanding sharply from late 2022. The homeowners policies still visible company by company in that tool fell from about 1.29 million at the end of 2022 to about 405,000 at the end of 2025, not because the market shrank, but because the insurers writing it went dark in that tool. Their statewide totals remain published; their county and zip-code detail does not. As reform moved policies off the state insurer, whose book is fully public at every level, they largely landed with trade secret filers.

The practical consequence: this page can tell you what the whole market pays, how big it is, and which carriers are growing or retreating statewide, all of which remain published insurer by insurer. What cannot be answered from public data is anything beneath the statewide line for most of the market: who writes coverage in a given county, where takeout policies actually landed, and how coverage terms compare, since deductibles and limits are published for no insurer at all. Citizens is the only insurer whose book is public at every level: monthly, by county, and with coverage detail.

Sources: s. 624.424(10)(b), Florida Statutes (2025) (aggregation and publication mandate); s. 624.4213, Florida Statutes (2025) (trade secret designation); FL OIR, Residential Market Share Reports (Trade Secret Companies list and statewide files); FL OIR Property Market Intelligence Report (Totals Report), reporting periods ending December 31, 2022 and December 31, 2025, generated through the OIR Market Intelligence interface, for the visible-policies figures. Calculation: Pioneer Institute.

Why does a smaller state insurer help you?

Citizens became Florida's largest home insurer during the crisis. Because of its surcharge authority, a large Citizens represents a risk to every policyholder. It is now smaller than at any point since before the pandemic. Source: Citizens, Policies in Force.
Figure 2 Homes insured by Citizens, the state insurer of last resort
The shaded band marks the period the main reforms passed. Citizens continued to grow for months afterward, then declined sharply as private insurers assumed policies. The series is month-end; Citizens' own releases cite a weekly peak of about 1.41 million in early October 2023. A smaller state insurer reduces the likelihood of a statewide surcharge.
How this is calculated
Exposure removed and change in size
exposure removed = $618,104,511,066 - $79,644,625,492 = $538,459,885,574 exposure change = -538,459,885,574 / 618,104,511,066 x 100 = -87.11% policies change = (278,246 - 1,407,805) / 1,407,805 x 100 = -80.24%
Result: $538.5 billion of catastrophe exposure removed, 87.1 percent; policies down 80.2 percent.
Inputs: Citizens Policies in Force and quarterly exposure filings.
And as it shrinks, the coverage Citizens keeps is getting more expensive per dollar of protection: each $100,000 of insured value went from $782 to $927 a year, up 18.5 percent, consistent with private carriers taking the easier risks and leaving the harder ones behind. Does a homeowners policy cover flooding? No, and it matters

Nearly half of Citizens' 2024 hurricane claims closed without payment, largely because the damage was flood.

Of the roughly 79,000 Citizens claims filed after hurricanes Debby, Helene, and Milton, 44 percent had closed without payment and 11 percent remained open. For Debby and Helene, only 24 and 28 percent of claims were paid, because most of the damage was flooding, which a standard homeowners policy excludes in every state. Citizens paid about $823 million on the season's claims overall.

Flood coverage requires a separate policy, through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private flood insurer, and typically carries a waiting period before it takes effect. A homeowner who drops wind coverage to cut cost and carries no flood policy retains very little hurricane protection at all.

Source: testimony of Citizens President/CEO Tim Cerio to the Florida Senate Banking and Insurance Committee, January 14, 2025, as reported by the Florida Phoenix. Claim counts and payment rates are Citizens figures presented in that testimony.

The reforms worked on the risk the state was carrying. They have not yet been shown to work on affordability, and the state does not measure it. Taxpayer catastrophe exposure is down 87 percent and the projected post-storm assessment is zero below a 1-in-360-year event. The market is profitable again, litigation has fallen sharply, and capital is returning. Three findings below qualify that conclusion: headline takeout numbers overstate progress until new policies entering are netted out; on Citizens' own shrinking book the price of coverage per dollar of protection rose even as average premiums fell, reflecting the harder risks left behind rather than market prices; and Florida publishes no coverage-adjusted affordability figure of its own. Measured from the state's aggregate files across every admitted insurer, the statewide price of protection has been flat since late 2023: a pause, not yet a decline. This view lays out what changed, what it cost, and what is still unmeasured.

Capital is entering, not merely returning.

Twenty-one new companies have been approved to write residential property in Florida since the reforms, bringing more than $850 million in new capital through May 2026. Competition is reappearing in the hardest niches: writers of wind-only condominium association coverage in Broward, Miami-Dade, and Palm Beach counties have grown from one to five. And the risk-transfer market has repriced Florida risk directly: Citizens' 2026 catastrophe bond, Everglades Re II 2026-1, was upsized by a third to $600 million and priced below guidance.

Sources: FL OIR Stability Report, Jul 1, 2026 (entrant count); FL OIR, May 20, 2026 (capital and condominium writers); Artemis, May 13, 2026 (catastrophe bond pricing).

What did the reforms actually change? Litigation

The exhibits above show outcomes. This one shows the mechanism the 2022 and 2023 reforms targeted. For a decade Florida generated a wildly disproportionate share of the nation's homeowners insurance lawsuits relative to its share of claims. That wedge is now closing.
Figure 2 Florida's share of nationwide homeowners suits versus its share of homeowners claims
In 2021, Florida produced 6.9 percent of the nation's homeowners claims and 76 percent of its homeowners suits. The preliminary 2025 figures are 4.9 percent of claims and 41.3 percent of suits, still disproportionate but the lowest suit share in the series; final 2025 data arrives in the third quarter of 2026. Defense and cost containment expense per claim fell from $947 in 2022 to $720 in 2025 on the restated basis of the 2025 annual statements. Source: FL OIR Property Insurance Stability Report, Jul 1, 2026, NAIC MCAS data and NAIC annual statement data.

How long does reform take to show up?

SB 2-A passed in December 2022. Citizens then grew for nine more months to an all-time peak in September 2023, and did not fall below its passage-day size until October 2024, twenty-two months later. Claims are governed by the law in force when the policy was written, so the pre-reform litigation tail runs for years.

At the twelve-month mark, every headline indicator still pointed the wrong way: Citizens was larger than at passage and still growing. The measured decline registered only in the second year.

Do gross takeout figures overstate progress?

The takeout number usually cited is the gross count: policies private carriers took from Citizens. It ignores the policies flowing in behind them, so a heavy takeout year can still end with Citizens larger than it started. Read the table left to right: what was taken out, what the book actually did, and the inflow that explains the difference.

The one-line version: in 2023 takeouts were more than offset by new arrivals and Citizens grew; by 2025 arrivals had nearly stopped; in the first half of 2026 Citizens shrank by more than its takeouts.

A further caution: any single quarter misleads

Assumption rounds pause during the heart of hurricane season and resume in the fall. The result is a predictable pattern: the book grows or flattens each summer and declines sharply each autumn. The autumn declines are also deepening.
Figure 4 Month-over-month change in Citizens policies. Bars below the line are months it shrank
A third-quarter reading appears as stagnation and a fourth-quarter reading as rapid progress, reflecting the seasonal pause and resumption of assumption rounds rather than the underlying trend. The autumn decline grew from about 179,000 in late 2023 to about 374,000 in late 2025. The trajectory is best assessed on twelve-month movements rather than quarters.

Constituent sentiment

A July 2026 Tampa focus group found affordability dominating every discussion, with insurance described as "just as burdensome as mortgage payments." Participants were broadly frustrated and skeptical of positive top-line messaging, while giving state leadership a passing grade overall.

"Working just to afford basic living means you can't actually enjoy living."

The systemic figures above and that frustration are not in conflict. The state balance sheet improved materially. Household costs improved late, modestly, and unevenly, and part of the apparent improvement reflects coverage reduction rather than price relief.

Source: Pioneer Institute focus group, Tampa, July 2, 2026. Quotations are from focus group participants.

About this tool. Florida Insurance Watch is a Pioneer Institute DataLabs research tool, published in beta and versioned. Corrections and data refreshes are logged in the changelog above, and the version and data vintage should be cited alongside any figure taken from this page. It is a living data tool, not a static report.
Research and educational use only. This tool is provided strictly for research and educational purposes. It does not constitute financial, legal, insurance, tax, actuarial, or investment advice, it creates no professional or advisory relationship, and it should not be relied upon for any coverage, purchasing, financial, or legal decision. Data sources, accuracy, and revisions. Figures are compiled in good faith from public filings issued by third parties, principally Citizens Property Insurance Corporation and the Florida Office of Insurance Regulation, and are accurate to the stated vintage at the time of verification. Those bodies publish on their own schedules and may revise, restate, or withdraw prior figures without notice; all figures here are point-in-time and may not match a source that has since been updated. The same measure is sometimes reported on different bases or as of different dates: for example, gross assumptions are stated as of June 16, 2026 against policies in force as of June 30, and the combined ratio is the weighted average OIR now uses, where its earlier reports used a straight average. This tool and its contents are provided as is, without warranty of any kind, express or implied, including accuracy, completeness, or fitness for a particular purpose.
Derived figures and model outputs. Measures labeled as Pioneer Institute calculations, including depopulation efficiency, implied inflow, price per $1,000 of insured value, and the market-wide premium and price-of-protection series, are derived from the cited filings, are shown with their arithmetic, and have not been reviewed or endorsed by the issuing agencies. Forward-looking figures are model outputs, not guarantees: in particular, post-storm outcomes are not predicted here, and the no-surcharge projection is a modeled annual figure that a sufficiently severe season would overturn.
Known limits of what this page can show. Figures are statewide and county averages, not quotes: an individual premium depends on the home, county, coverage, and insurer. A lower average premium can reflect reduced coverage rather than a lower price, so deductibles and wind coverage should be read alongside any premium figure. Causation is not established: the reform period coincided with a softening reinsurance market, a Cat Fund rate reduction, and quiet storm seasons, and separating reform from cycle requires a counterfactual this page does not attempt. Premium in force is a volume measure, not a price. Non-renewals, exits from the insured market, and the uninsured share are not confirmed against primary sources and are excluded from analysis.
