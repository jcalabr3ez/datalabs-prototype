Florida Insurance Watch

What Florida households pay for homeowners insurance, what their coverage now includes, and how both have changed. Compiled from state filings.

Premiums remain high even as the market stabilizes. Both facts are accurate. Affordability was the dominant theme in a July 2026 Tampa focus group, where participants described their premiums as "just as burdensome as mortgage payments." The market did stabilize and average rates have begun to fall. Florida nonetheless remains the most expensive state in the country, the relief arrived late and is small against several years of increases, and part of the decline reflects reduced coverage rather than lower prices. This page sets out what changed, drawn from state filings, and what it means for a household.

Why have premiums risen despite reports of market improvement?

Both are accurate, and they are consistent.

The reforms addressed what they were designed to address: the market stopped contracting, insurers re-entered, and the state moved away from a near-term solvency crisis. That progress is the reason rate decreases have replaced double-digit increases.

Rate relief nonetheless arrived late and modest relative to the preceding increases. Between 2019 and 2022 the average Florida premium rose from about $1,988 to $2,677, while the national average rose more slowly, from $1,272 to $1,569. An 8.8 percent reduction in 2026 does not offset several years of increases, so premiums are below the prior year yet remain well above earlier levels.

A second factor complicates the averages. A decline in average premium can indicate lower prices, or reduced purchase of coverage through higher deductibles, lower limits, or removal of wind coverage. Measured per $1,000 of coverage, the price of Citizens coverage rose about 18 percent from 2023 to 2026, even as average premium per policy fell.

Sources: premium run-up, NAIC average HO-3 premium by state, 2019 and 2022, via the Insurance Information Institute; price per $1,000 of coverage, Pioneer calculation from Citizens premium and exposure filings.

Are premiums falling, or is less coverage being purchased?

The exhibit compares two measures, both indexed to 100 in 2023: the average premium a policyholder pays, and the price for each $1,000 of coverage. The two move in opposite directions.
Figure 1 Average premium versus the price of coverage, both set to 100 in 2023
The average premium fell about 23 percent, largely because policyholders purchased less coverage through higher deductibles, lower limits, and in some cases no wind coverage. The price for each $1,000 of coverage rose about 18 percent over the same period. A lower premium accompanied by reduced coverage falls in the gap between these two lines. Over the same period the average amount of coverage carried per policy fell about 35 percent, a steeper decline than the premium itself. Source: Citizens premium and coverage filings. ▶Why some questions cannot be answered yet
Florida maintains a public market report intended to reflect the entire homeowners market. It no longer does so, a limitation relevant to any claim about coverage across the market.

Insurers are allowed to mark their data a trade secret, so it is withheld from the public report. As reform moved policies off the state insurer and onto private carriers, a larger share of the market became unreported. The homeowners policies visible in that report fell from about 1.29 million at the end of 2022 to about 405,000 at the end of 2025, even though the real market did not shrink that way.

As a result, any statement about coverage across the full market now draws on a report that captures less than half of it. The only consistent, like-for-like coverage measure on this page is the Citizens book above, a single insurer reported on the same basis each quarter.

Source: FL OIR Property Market Intelligence Report (Totals Report), reporting periods ending December 31, 2022 and December 31, 2025, generated through the OIR Market Intelligence interface; individual filings withheld as trade secrets under s. 624.4213, Florida Statutes; the reporting mandate and the statewide-aggregation requirement sit in s. 624.424(10). Calculation: Pioneer Institute.

Why does a smaller state insurer help you?

Citizens became Florida's largest home insurer during the crisis. Because of its surcharge authority, a large Citizens represents a risk to every policyholder. It is now smaller than at any point since before the pandemic. Source: Citizens, Policies in Force.
Figure 2 Homes insured by Citizens, the state insurer of last resort
The shaded band marks the period the main reforms passed. Citizens continued to grow for months afterward, then declined sharply as private insurers assumed policies. The series is month-end; Citizens' own releases cite a weekly peak of about 1.41 million in early October 2023. A smaller state insurer reduces the likelihood of a statewide surcharge.

Result: $538.5 billion of catastrophe exposure removed, 87.1 percent; policies down 80.2 percent.
Inputs: Citizens Policies in Force and quarterly exposure filings.
And as it shrinks, the coverage Citizens keeps is getting more expensive per dollar of protection: its price per $1,000 of insured value rose from $7.82 to $9.27, up 18.5 percent, consistent with private carriers taking the easier risks and leaving the harder ones behind. Source: Citizens Property Insurance Corporation, Policies in Force; price per $1,000 is a Pioneer Institute calculation from Citizens exposure and premium filings.

Does a homeowners policy cover flooding? No, and it matters

Nearly half of Citizens' 2024 hurricane claims closed without payment, largely because the damage was flood.

Of the roughly 79,000 Citizens claims filed after hurricanes Debby, Helene, and Milton, 44 percent had closed without payment and 11 percent remained open. For Debby and Helene, only 24 and 28 percent of claims were paid, because most of the damage was flooding, which a standard homeowners policy excludes in every state. Citizens paid about $823 million on the season's claims overall.

Flood coverage requires a separate policy, through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private flood insurer, and typically carries a waiting period before it takes effect. A homeowner who drops wind coverage to cut cost and carries no flood policy retains very little hurricane protection at all.

Source: testimony of Citizens President/CEO Tim Cerio to the Florida Senate Banking and Insurance Committee, January 14, 2025, as reported by the Florida Phoenix. Claim counts and payment rates are Citizens figures presented in that testimony.
▶What this page cannot tell you
  1. An individual premium. These are statewide and county figures. A specific premium depends on the home, county, coverage, and insurer.
  2. Whether lower cost means better value. A lower average premium can mask reduced coverage; deductibles and wind coverage matter alongside the total premium.
  3. Post-storm outcomes. The no-surcharge projection is a model, not a guarantee.
  4. Prototype status. Figures are drawn from public state filings and do not constitute financial or insurance advice.

The reforms worked on the risk the state was carrying. They have not yet been shown to work on affordability, and the state does not measure it. Taxpayer catastrophe exposure is down 87 percent and the projected post-storm assessment is zero below a 1-in-360-year event. The market is profitable again, litigation has fallen sharply, and capital is returning. Three findings below qualify that conclusion: headline takeout numbers overstate progress until new policies entering are netted out, the price of coverage per dollar of protection has risen even as average premiums fell, and Florida publishes no coverage-adjusted affordability figure, so no one can say whether protection actually became cheaper. This view lays out what changed, what it cost, and what is still unmeasured.

How healthy is the market?

Figure 1 Combined ratio, Florida domestic insurers. Below 100 is an underwriting profit
Claims plus expenses over premiums. Florida domestics moved from deep loss in 2020 to profit in 2023 and 83 percent in 2025, the lowest in more than a decade and the precondition for rate relief rather than evidence of it. Capital is entering, not merely returning.

Twenty-one new companies have been approved to write residential property in Florida since the reforms, bringing more than $850 million in new capital. Competition is reappearing in the hardest niches: writers of wind-only condominium association coverage in Broward, Miami-Dade, and Palm Beach counties have grown from one to five. And the risk-transfer market has repriced Florida risk directly: Citizens' 2026 catastrophe bond, Everglades Re II 2026-1, was upsized by a third to $600 million and priced below guidance.

Sources: FL OIR Stability Report, Jul 1, 2026 (entrant count); FL OIR, May 20, 2026 (capital and condominium writers); Artemis, May 13, 2026 (catastrophe bond pricing).

What did the reforms actually change? Litigation

The exhibits above show outcomes. This one shows the mechanism the 2022 and 2023 reforms targeted. For a decade Florida generated a wildly disproportionate share of the nation's homeowners insurance lawsuits relative to its share of claims. That wedge is now closing.
Figure 2 Florida's share of nationwide homeowners suits versus its share of homeowners claims
In 2021, Florida produced 6.9 percent of the nation's homeowners claims and 76 percent of its homeowners suits. The preliminary 2025 figures are 4.9 percent of claims and 41.3 percent of suits, still disproportionate but the lowest suit share in the series; final 2025 data arrives in the third quarter of 2026. Defense and cost containment expense per claim fell from $947 in 2022 to $720 in 2025 on the restated basis of the 2025 annual statements. Source: FL OIR Property Insurance Stability Report, Jul 1, 2026, NAIC MCAS data and NAIC annual statement data.

How long does reform take to show up?

SB 2-A passed in December 2022. Citizens then grew for nine more months to an all-time peak in September 2023, and did not fall below its passage-day size until October 2024, twenty-two months later. Claims are governed by the law in force when the policy was written, so the pre-reform litigation tail runs for years.

Implication for states considering reform: a legislature evaluating its reform at twelve months would conclude it had failed, and could begin reversing it at the point it begins to take effect.

Do gross takeout figures overstate progress?

Citizens reports how many policies private carriers take out, but not how many enter behind them. Gross takeouts are the figure usually cited, and they overstate net progress: a heavy takeout year can still end with a larger book. A further caution: any single quarter misleads

Assumption rounds pause during the heart of hurricane season and resume in the fall. The result is a predictable pattern: the book grows or flattens each summer and declines sharply each autumn. The autumn declines are also deepening.

What should Florida do next?

Five recommendations. The first two close gaps in what the state measures and publishes; the last three define what a finished job looks like, so the gains are secured rather than reversed. These are Pioneer Institute recommendations offered for debate, not figures drawn from a state filing.
1

Publish a coverage-adjusted statewide premium

Do this: require the state to publish one statewide premium figure that adjusts for how much coverage people actually buy.

Right now the state publishes an average premium for each of the 67 counties and no statewide number at all, and the national series stops at 2022. So during the biggest insurance reform in the country, nobody can say what the average Floridian actually paid in 2023, 2024 or 2025. Worse, a plain average can fall for the wrong reason. If people raise their deductible and drop wind coverage, the average bill goes down while the protection goes down further. An adjusted figure prices a fixed amount of coverage, so it shows whether insurance got cheaper or just smaller.

For example: on the state insurer's book, the average premium per policy fell about 23 percent from the 2023 peak, which suggests substantial relief. The price for each $1,000 of coverage rose 18.5 percent over the same period. Both figures are accurate; only the second measures the cost of a fixed amount of protection.

2

Restore visibility into the full market

Do this: confirm the state's market report is publishing the statewide totals the law already requires, then publish a consistent panel of insurers over time.

Florida runs a public report meant to cover the entire homeowners market. Insurers may withhold their own numbers as a trade secret, and as reform moved policies from the state insurer to private companies, a larger share of the market became unreported. This is primarily a question of oversight rather than new legislation. Section 624.424(10)(b) already requires the state to aggregate those filings statewide, publish the aggregate within one month, and provides that the statewide aggregate is not a trade secret. The first question is whether the published report satisfies that requirement.

For example: the homeowners policies visible in that public report fell from about 1.29 million at the end of 2022 to about 405,000 at the end of 2025. Florida's housing market did not contract by two-thirds. Those policies were not withdrawn; they ceased to be reported, so any characterization of the full market now rests on a report covering less than half of it.

3

Define a target size for the state insurer

Do this: define a market-share band the state insurer should stay inside, and manage to it in both directions.

The state insurer exists as a last resort, and its size is a rough gauge of whether the private market is working. It has contracted substantially, but there is no stated target, so nobody can say whether it is now right-sized or has overshot. A band matters in both directions: a floor stops it being run down past the point where it can do its job, and a ceiling requires action before it expands again in the next hard market.

For example: the state insurer's share of homeowners policies fell from 8.81 percent in September 2025 to 1.93 percent by March 2026. Without a defined band, the next time it grows, one side will call it proof the reforms failed and the other will call it noise, and neither will have a benchmark to point to.

4

Make the assessment protection permanent

Do this: write a minimum storm-severity standard into law, so no post-storm charge can be levied for anything short of it.

After a bad storm the state insurer can add a charge to almost every insurance policy in Florida, including auto, and including policies held by people who are not its customers. Today the protection against that is real but temporary: it depends on the reinsurance bought this year and is re-earned every season. Putting a floor in statute means the protection survives a hard reinsurance market and a bad hurricane year instead of quietly eroding.

For example: the state insurer currently projects no charge unless a storm worse than a 1-in-360-year event hits. A statutory 1-in-100 floor would secure protection well inside what has already been achieved, making it a conservative standard rather than an aspirational one.

5

Preserve the litigation and eligibility reforms

Do this: protect the 2022 and 2023 litigation and eligibility changes from rollback while the effects are still working through.

The improvement is not self-sustaining, and the timing creates a risk of premature reversal. Reform took about 22 months to show up in the numbers, and claims are governed by the law in force when the policy was written, so pre-reform costs continue to work through the system for years. That leaves an extended period in which the reforms may be judged complete and relaxed while still taking effect.

For example: in 2023, for every 100 policies private carriers took off the state insurer, about 130 new ones came in behind them, so the book grew during a record takeout year. The trend reversed only after the underlying cost drivers changed; reversing those measures would restore the same dynamic.

One objective is substantially met; the other is not.

On the question the reforms were built to answer, whether the state was carrying too much hurricane risk, the work is nearly complete: taxpayer exposure is down 87 percent and no post-storm charge is projected below a 1-in-360-year storm. On affordability, the work is at an early stage and the state does not yet measure it. Defining the finish line is also what protects the gains, because a reform with no stated endpoint can be unwound before it is secure.

Recommendations are Pioneer Institute framing, offered for debate; target values are illustrative starting points, not settled positions. What are the limitations?

  1. Causation is not established. The period coincided with a softening reinsurance market (preliminary 2026 data show risk-adjusted reinsurance costs down 15 to 25 percent for nearly half of insurers), a 14.0 percent Cat Fund rate reduction for the 2025 contract year (the 2026 contract year rate change is an increase of 0.43 percent), and a 2025 season with no Florida landfall. Separating reform from cycle needs a counterfactual; Louisiana is the natural comparison.
  2. Rate relief is real but modest. Filed rates turned negative before charged premiums did, and the cuts are small against the 2019 to 2022 run-up.
  3. Premium in force is volume, not price. It fell because the book contracted, a distinct fact from the rate reduction.
  4. Exit from the market is not captured. Non-renewals and the uninsured share are not confirmed against primary sources and are excluded from analysis.

Constituent sentiment

A July 2026 Tampa focus group found affordability dominating every discussion, with insurance described as "just as burdensome as mortgage payments." Participants were broadly frustrated and skeptical of positive top-line messaging, while giving state leadership a passing grade overall.

"Working just to afford basic living means you can't actually enjoy living."

The systemic figures above and that frustration are not in conflict. The state balance sheet improved materially. Household costs improved late, modestly, and unevenly, and part of the apparent improvement reflects coverage reduction rather than price relief. Messaging that declares affordability resolved is unlikely to align with constituent experience; messaging that the market has stabilized and rate relief is beginning, with further work required, is more defensible.

Data accuracy and discrepancies. The figures on this page are compiled in good faith from public filings issued by third parties, principally Citizens Property Insurance Corporation and the Florida Office of Insurance Regulation. Those bodies publish on their own schedules and may revise, restate, or withdraw prior figures without notice. The same measure is sometimes reported on different bases or as of different dates: for example, gross assumptions here are stated as of June 16, 2026 against policies in force as of June 30, and the combined ratio is the weighted average OIR now uses, where its earlier reports used a straight average. Derived measures, including depopulation efficiency and price per $1,000 of insured value, are Pioneer Institute's own calculations and have not been reviewed or endorsed by the issuing agencies. All figures are point-in-time and may not match a source that has since been updated.
