Is the T back to pre-pandemic ridership?

One line answers it: every trip taken on the system, month by month, since 2014. The pandemic crater is the deepest point in the record, and the climb back is real but incomplete. The dashed line marks the 2019 monthly average, the level the system has not yet reached again.
Figure 1 MBTA monthly ridership, all modes, 2014 to June 2026
Unlinked passenger trips across every MBTA mode. April 2020, the pandemic floor, saw 4,297,427 trips, about 13.7 percent of the same month a year earlier (derived, SRC-301). The recovery has been steady, and June 2026 set the post-pandemic record: 26,288,869 trips against 29,854,610 in June 2019 puts the system at 88.1 percent of its pre-pandemic level (derived vs same month 2019, SRC-301), the strongest month since March 2020.
How this is calculated
Recovery vs same month 2019 (the measure used throughout this page)
recovery = month / same month 2019 x 100 June 2026: 26,288,869 / 29,854,610 x 100 = 88.1% year over year: 26,288,869 / 22,281,887 x 100 - 100 = +18.0%
Result: 88.1 percent of pre-pandemic ridership, growing 18.0 percent year over year.
Inputs: FTA NTD Complete Monthly Ridership, dataset 8bui-9xvu, data.transportation.gov, fetched and re-verified against the live dataset August 11, 2026. Same-month comparison avoids seasonal distortion. Calculation: Pioneer Institute.

Which riders came back first?

The average hides two different recoveries. Each bar is a mode’s June 2026 ridership as a share of its own June 2019 level; the gold line marks a full return.
Figure 2 Recovery vs the same month in 2019, by mode
Ferry leads at 118.9 percent of its 2019 level and commuter rail stands at 107.7 percent, both above pre-pandemic; bus rapid transit and bus are nearly back at 98.4 and 97.2 percent. Heavy rail, the subway that anchors the system, sits at 79.1 percent, and The RIDE, the paratransit service, is at 69.7 percent (derived vs same month 2019, SRC-301). Read together: the suburban and occasional-trip services recovered first, and the everyday subway core is what remains missing.
Source: FTA NTD Complete Monthly Ridership (SRC-301), June 2026 vs June 2019, derived. Calculation: Pioneer Institute.

How many riders does each hour of service carry?

Productivity is unlinked trips divided by vehicle revenue hours, the riders carried for each hour a vehicle is in service. Each mode is shown at its 2019 level and its 2025 level; where the 2025 bar is shorter, the same service now carries fewer riders.
Figure 3 Unlinked trips per vehicle revenue hour, 2019 vs 2025, by mode
Heavy rail fell furthest, from 109.7 trips per revenue hour in 2019 to 64.5 in 2025, a drop of 41.2 percent (derived, SRC-301). Light rail and commuter rail also carry fewer riders per service hour than before the pandemic, while bus and bus rapid transit are essentially unchanged. The RIDE, a door-to-door paratransit service, carries close to one trip per revenue hour by design and is left off the scale so the fixed-route modes stay readable.
Source: FTA NTD Complete Monthly Ridership (SRC-301), unlinked trips and vehicle revenue hours, full years 2019 and 2025. Calculation: Pioneer Institute.

Where has service outrun ridership?

Each mode is shown twice as a share of its own 2019 level: the service the MBTA ran in 2025, and the riders it carried. Where the service bar clears one hundred but the ridership bar does not, the T is running as much or more than before for a smaller crowd.
Figure 4 Service supplied and ridership in 2025, as a share of 2019, by mode
Commuter rail, ferry, and heavy rail all ran more service in 2025 than in 2019, yet only commuter rail came close to matching its former ridership (derived vs full-year 2019, SRC-301). Bus and bus rapid transit are the exception: service and ridership fell together, so their productivity held steady. Systemwide, service stood at 89.9 percent of 2019 and ridership at 74.2 percent, a fifteen point gap that lands directly on cost per trip.
Source: FTA NTD Complete Monthly Ridership (SRC-301), full years 2025 and 2019, derived. Calculation: Pioneer Institute.

How reliable is the T?

What reliability means here. Reliability is the share of trips that met the MBTA’s headway or schedule-adherence standard, from the MBTA Open Data Portal (SRC-303). Figures cover the trailing twelve months ending at each mode’s latest published month. Bus, commuter rail, and The RIDE are volume weighted; ferry is an unweighted mean of line-level on-time performance. Subway and Green Line are not shown: the MBTA measures rapid transit with Excess Trip Time, a different method adopted December 2024 that is not comparable to this headway series.

Which modes run on time?

Each bar is the share of trips that met the MBTA’s reliability standard over the trailing twelve months. Ferry and commuter rail clear ninety percent; bus, the workhorse of the network, sits near seventy.
Figure 5 Reliability by mode, trailing twelve months
Ferry leads at 98.7 percent and The RIDE follows at 94.2 percent, then commuter rail at 89.9 percent and bus at 70.2 percent (SRC-303). The spread is wide because the modes run on different service patterns: a scheduled boat or train is judged against a timetable, while a frequent bus is judged against its headway, the gap between buses. Subway and Green Line are excluded for the reasons noted above.
Source: MBTA Open Data Portal, service reliability datasets (SRC-303). Calculation: Pioneer Institute.

Is reliability getting better or worse?

Bus and commuter rail are the two modes with a clean record back to 2016. Both improved during the low-traffic pandemic years and have drifted back toward their pre-pandemic levels since.
Figure 6 Bus and commuter rail reliability by year, 2016 to 2026
Both modes ran more reliably in 2020 and 2021, when light roads and empty trains made schedules easier to keep, then eased back as traffic returned (SRC-303). Commuter rail has held in the low nineties; bus has stayed near seventy for a decade. The 2026 points cover a partial year. Reliability by individual line and route is below.
Source: MBTA Open Data Portal, bus and commuter rail reliability (SRC-303). Calculation: Pioneer Institute.

What does a trip cost to provide?

Fares are what riders pay; these figures are what the service costs to operate, per trip taken. They are computed from FTA NTD Annual Metrics, report years 2022 through 2024, as operating expenses per unlinked trip and fare revenues over operating expenses.
Cost figures are verified. Cost per trip and farebox recovery are computed from FTA NTD Annual Metrics (SRC-302), report years 2022 to 2024. The recovered legacy extract (LEG-MBTA-01) that first carried these figures was reconciled on Aug 11, 2026: it matched report year 2024 exactly on all eight mode rows, which both dated the extract and retired its pending flag. Ridership figures are separately verified against the federal monthly file (SRC-301).
Figure 7 Operating cost per unlinked trip, by mode
Heavy rail is the cheapest service to provide per rider at $4.80 a trip, and commuter rail the most expensive of the fixed-route modes at $19.51 (SRC-302, report year 2024). The RIDE, the door-to-door paratransit service, costs $161.25 per trip and is left off the scale so the other modes stay readable. Contracted services are marked; they are operated for the MBTA by private providers. Across 2022 to 2024, cost per trip rose 13.5 percent on heavy rail while falling 41.1 percent on commuter rail as its ridership recovered (derived, SRC-302).
Source: FTA NTD Annual Metrics, report years 2022 to 2024 (SRC-302); operating expenses per unlinked trip.

What share of the cost do fares cover?

Farebox recovery is the share of operating cost paid by the people riding. Every point below one hundred is covered by taxpayers, which is what makes this the central policy number in any debate about fares, service, and subsidy.
Figure 8 Farebox recovery, by mode
No MBTA mode comes close to paying for itself at the farebox. Ferry leads at 35.8 percent and heavy rail follows at 33.7 percent; bus riders cover 12.3 percent of the cost of their trips, and The RIDE covers 1.8 percent (SRC-302, report year 2024). Recovery slipped on the subway across the series, from 37.5 percent in 2022 to 33.7 percent in 2024, as costs grew faster than fare revenue (derived, SRC-302).
Source: FTA NTD Annual Metrics, report years 2022 to 2024 (SRC-302); fare revenues over operating expenses.
▶What this page does not cover

This tool covers MBTA ridership as unlinked passenger trips, the service supplied and its productivity, reliability for bus, commuter rail, ferry, and The RIDE, and per-trip cost and farebox recovery, all by mode. It does not cover subway or Green Line reliability, which the MBTA measures with Excess Trip Time on a different basis, nor safety, the capital budget, debt, the fares charged to riders, or other transit agencies. Questions on those subjects asked through the DataLabs front door are declined rather than improvised, and every one is logged and informs what gets verified and added next.
