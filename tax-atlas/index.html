Before the November election
Aug. 30, 2026NYPied-a-terre owner notices due; first compliance deadline of the new tax.

New York enacted an annual surcharge on NYC residences that are not the owner’s primary home, effective July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2031, projected at roughly $500 million per year from about 10,000 to 13,000 properties. Condos and co-ops assessed at $1 million or more pay 4%, 5.25%, or 6.5% by tier; one-to-three-family homes at $5 million or more pay 0.8% to 1.3%. Owners must file notices establishing primary-residence status by Aug. 30; the Department of Finance finalized its implementation rules on July 14, 2026, covering residency determinations and audits, and exemption application deadlines were extended to Sept. 18. Why it matters: this is the first big-city tax keyed to property ownership rather than income, a template other cities are watching, and its rollout will show whether valuation and residency disputes make wealth-adjacent taxes administrable. Legal exposure is now live: on Aug. 7, 2026, three homeowners represented by former Deputy Mayor Randy Mastro sued in Richmond County Supreme Court over the rollout, and on Aug. 10 Justice Wayne M. Ozzi issued a temporary restraining order halting the published property list, further notices, and collection steps; the city’s appeal stays the order, and a hearing is set for Aug. 31, one day after the notice deadline.

Wed., Sept. 9, 2026RIDemocratic gubernatorial primary.

Rhode Island enacted a phased millionaires surtax in June (1% on income over $1M in 2027, rising to 3% by 2029, top rate to 8.99%) plus a 0.5% annual surcharge on non-owner-occupied homes assessed over $1M. The primary decides whether the party’s nominee comes from the wing that treated the enacted surtax as the compromise or the wing backing the broader Fair Share package (H 7313 / S 2238: a 3% surtax starting at $640K plus a 1% annual tax on financial assets over $25M, which would be the first true state wealth tax in America). Why it matters: the winner shapes whether Rhode Island’s 2027 session stops at the enacted phase-in or reopens the wealth tax question.

Sept. 2026MASecretary’s voter information booklet publishes for all nine certified questions.

Nine statewide questions were certified in July for the Nov. 3 ballot, tying the modern record set in 1972, 1976, and 1994, including the Chapter 62F rewrite (Question 5). The September booklet mails the official summaries and for/against arguments to every household, the single largest voter touchpoint before election day. Why it matters: 62F polling is soft and low-information; the official framing that lands in every mailbox materially shapes the outcome.

Late 2026 (no date set)WAPetter v. Dept. of Revenue trial ruling, Klickitat County Superior Court.

The issue: whether Washington’s new 9.9% tax on income over $1M (SB 6346, effective 2028) violates the state constitution. Under Culliton v. Chase (1933), income is property in Washington, and the constitution requires property to be taxed uniformly and caps it at 1%, which is why the state has never had a graduated income tax. Plaintiffs (business owners and trade associations, represented by former Attorney General Rob McKenna and former Justice Phil Talmadge) seek a declaratory judgment voiding the tax; the state will argue it is an excise, the theory that saved the capital gains tax in 2023. Potential impact: if struck down, roughly $3 billion per year in projected revenue from an estimated 21,000 filers disappears and the 90-year wall against graduated income taxation holds, chilling copycats in Oregon and beyond. If upheld, the wall falls in the state that built it. No ruling reported as of August 11, 2026; final resolution expected at the state Supreme Court in early 2027.

Election Day: November 3, 2026
Nov. 3, 2026CAProposition 40 and Proposition 3 both resolve.

Prop 40 is a one-time 5% tax on net worth above $1 billion, covering resident individuals and applicable trusts, structured as a combined constitutional amendment and statute, the only true wealth tax on any American ballot, qualified with 980,438 valid signatures. Real estate, pensions, and retirement accounts are excluded; payment is due in 2027 or in five-year installments. Building a Better California, the opposition committee co-founded by Sergey Brin and Eric Schmidt, has reported more than $118 million raised, including $102 million contributed personally by Brin per filings disclosed through Aug. 7, 2026; supporters report just over $30 million, and the California Democratic Party endorsed the measure in a narrow executive-board vote in early August. Prop 3, sharing the ballot, would make the Prop 30-era top income brackets (top combined rate 13.3%) permanent past their scheduled expiration after tax year 2030. Potential impact: a Prop 40 win is the first voter-approved wealth tax in US history and the national template; a loss after $80M+ against it becomes the cautionary tale every other state coalition must answer. Either way, the result recalibrates every state’s risk overnight.

Nov. 3, 2026WAInitiative 645: repeal of the millionaires tax.

Certified for the ballot on July 15, 2026 with more than 500,000 signatures against a 308,911 requirement, I-645 (Let’s Go Washington) would repeal SB 6346 and prohibit state and local taxes on individual income. Why it matters: this is the direct democratic verdict on the tax, running in parallel with the Petter litigation; repeal moots the court fight, while a failed repeal strengthens the state’s position and the national contagion argument. The ballot-language fight is already in court: on Aug. 7, 2026 a Thurston County judge upheld the government-drafted fiscal-impact disclosure that will appear beside the measure, and an emergency appeal reached the state Supreme Court on Aug. 10, racing the Aug. 21 county ballot-finalization deadline.

Nov. 3, 2026MAThe 62F tax-cap question (Question 5).

Chapter 62F is the 1986 law that refunds taxpayers when state tax collections grow faster than wages; it triggered in 2022, returning about $3 billion. The ballot question rewrites the formula two ways: it rebases the cap to actual collections and pulls Fair Share surtax revenue into the capped base. Modeled effect (MTF): the cap would have triggered in four of the last ten years, with refunds of roughly $269 to $1,095 per filer. Potential impact: passage converts the surtax’s over-performance into automatic refund pressure, effectively a second round of the Fair Share fight; defeat leaves the surtax revenue fully outside the cap and removes the main structural check on its growth. A second front opened in late July: the state Senate voted 31–8 on July 23, 2026 to fold a 62F rewrite into the economic development bill, replacing the cap formula with a trigger at 7.5% of statewide personal income; the provision sits in a House-Senate conference as of Aug. 11, and if enacted it would complicate or moot the ballot question.

Nov. 3, 2026WAFive of nine state Supreme Court seats on the ballot.

A wave of retirements and appointments put a majority of the court’s seats up in one cycle. Whoever wins will almost certainly decide the Petter appeal in 2027, the final ruling on whether the millionaires tax survives. Potential impact: this is the rare election where judicial races directly determine a multi-billion-dollar tax outcome; both sides’ spending and candidate positioning on Culliton-era precedent are the tells to watch.

Nov. 3, 2026VTLegislative races.

Vermont’s 2026 wealth-tax package (investment income taxes plus a new top-1% bracket, roughly $170M per year by 2028 per reported committee materials) died because Gov. Scott opposed it and Democrats lost their veto-proof supermajority in 2024. The package is pre-built and legislators expect the debate to resume in 2027. Potential impact: a restored two-thirds supermajority removes the only binding constraint; Vermont could then enact over a veto in the 2027 session, making these down-ballot races one of the highest-leverage tax events in the country.

Nov. 3, 2026CTGovernorship and both chambers.

Gov. Lamont has publicly opposed income tax rate increases and no high-income bill advanced in 2026, but the legislature’s progressive caucus has pushed surtax proposals repeatedly, and a statewide mansion tax proposal surfaced this year (Foley Wealth Tax Watch). Potential impact: Connecticut is a one-election state; a governor aligned with the caucus converts blocked proposals into live vehicles in 2027.

Nov. 3, 2026NCConstitutional amendment capping the income tax rate at 3.5%.

North Carolina’s 2026 budget (S.L. 2026-41 / S.B. 257, Sec. 44.1, signed July 7, 2026) rewrote G.S. 105-153.7 to replace the trigger-dependent schedule with date-certain cuts: 3.99% for 2026, 3.49% for tax years 2027 to 2029, 3.24% for 2030 to 2032, and 2.99% after 2032, with revenue triggers permitting further quarter-point reductions to a 2.49% floor. The companion legislatively referred amendment, approved by both chambers in May 2026, would lower the constitutional income tax cap from 7% (approved by voters in 2018) to 3.5%; a second referred amendment on the same ballot would limit local property tax increases. Why it matters here: a 3.5% ceiling would make North Carolina the largest state with a hard constitutional bar against any future high-earner bracket, converting the cut-side divergence into constitutional text.

Nov. 3, 2026 (MO: RESOLVED Aug. 4)UT·ND·SD·MOFour states vote on raising their own initiative thresholds; Missouri’s failed.

Four states put measures before voters making their citizen-initiative processes harder to use (higher signature requirements or approval thresholds; Utah’s SJR 2 targets tax measures specifically). RESOLVED (MO): on Aug. 4, 2026, Missouri voters decisively rejected Amendment 4, which would have required citizen-initiated constitutional amendments to also carry a majority in every congressional district, 80.3% to 19.7%; all eight congressional districts voted no, and the companion Amendment 5, which would have created a path toward eliminating the individual income tax, also failed with 83.3% opposed (Missouri Independent, Aug. 4–5; Springfield Daily Citizen, Aug. 6, 2026). Utah, North Dakota, and South Dakota vote Nov. 3. Why it matters here: the initiative pathway is how wealth taxes reach voters where legislatures will not act, California’s Prop 40 route. Every state that raises its threshold closes that door a little, and Missouri’s emphatic rejection is the first 2026 data point that voters will defend the open door; the November results reshape the Ballot Access layer of the map and the long-run geography of where these fights can even occur.

2027
Jan. 1, 2027RISurtax phase one begins collecting: 1% on income over $1M.

The first new-state surtax collection since Massachusetts. First-year revenue against the roughly $142M full-phase-in estimate becomes the next data point in the national argument, and the compliance experience (estimated payments, withholding on the new bracket) previews the 2% and 3% steps.

Jan. 1, 2027HIThe 13% bracket on income over $1M (joint) takes effect (Act 24).

Hawaii becomes the second-highest state rate after California’s 13.3%. Watch first-year filer behavior in a state with unusual migration friction (an island economy) and whether the dormant Rhoads wealth tax bill (1% on net assets over $20M) is refiled on the strength of the new bracket’s revenue.

Jan. 1, 2027NE·MT·IN·MSCut-side steps take effect: NE 3.99%, MT 5.4%, IN 2.9%, MS 3.75%.

The other half of the national divergence: enacted, date-certain reductions in four states the same day two states raise. The widening spread between the two Americas is the backdrop for every migration and competitiveness argument in the raising states.

Jan.-Feb. 2027VADoes the Democratic trifecta refile the high-income bills.

Virginia became a full Democratic trifecta in January 2026 when Gov. Spanberger took office. HB 979 (new 8% and 10% brackets at $600K and $1M) and HB 378 (a 3.8% net investment income tax) were filed in 2026 and died at the March adjournment; the governor neither endorsed nor pledged to sign them, and state revenues ran 2.9% ahead of forecast. Potential impact: refiling with leadership support in the roughly 46-day short session would make Virginia the first Southern state with a live millionaire-bracket vehicle; both chambers are on the Nov. 2027 ballot, which cuts both ways on appetite.

Jan.-May 2027VTDoes the wealth-tax package return, and with whose name on it.

The tell is sponsorship: a rank-and-file refile repeats 2026, but House or Senate leadership sponsorship signals a decision to spend capital, especially if November restored a supermajority. The package’s contents (investment income taxes plus a top-1% bracket) are already drafted.

Jan.-May 2027MNFirst high-income vehicle from the trifecta, or another quiet session.

Minnesota already taxes net investment income over $1M at 1% (enacted 2023, effective tax year 2024) and has the political composition to go further, but no bill has been filed. A filed vehicle moves it up the risk board immediately; another quiet session confirms the 2023 measure as the ceiling for now.

Early 2027WAPetter reaches the state Supreme Court.

The final constitutional test of the millionaires tax, decided by the bench the November elections just shaped, and mooted if I-645 repealed the tax. The question is whether the court reaffirms Culliton (income is property, graduated rates unconstitutional) or extends the 2023 excise reasoning to an income tax. Potential impact: the ruling either voids roughly $3B per year before first collections in 2029, or formally ends the constitutional barrier that has defined Washington tax policy since 1933, with immediate contagion effects on Oregon’s 2028 plans.

April 1, 2027NYFY2027-28 budget: does the NYC income surtax return.

Mamdani’s $5.5B package, including a 2% NYC surtax on high earners, was rejected in 2026; the pied-a-terre tax and state aid closed the gap instead. City budget documents project deficits in FY2028-29, and Gov. Hochul has publicly opposed income tax rate increases. Potential impact: the budget shows whether the pied-a-terre tax bought a durable truce or one year; a surtax revival against projected gaps would be the largest single high-earner tax fight in the country by dollar value.

Aug. 4, 2027MAInitiative filing deadline with the Attorney General (first Wednesday of August).

The gateway for the 2028 ballot. Watch for: a refiled income tax cut, which Republican gubernatorial candidate Brian Shortsleeve has publicly vowed to lead for 2028 after the SJC removed the 2026 version (State House News, June 2026; under the Article 48 calendar no 2028 petition can be filed before this 2027 window, and none had been filed as of Aug. 11, 2026); and any statutory filing on capital gains rates or the estate tax, the two expansion routes that remain open without a constitutional amendment (the surtax itself cannot be expanded before a 2030 amendment ballot at the earliest). Potential impact: what gets filed here defines the entire 2028 Massachusetts fight.

Oct. 2027MAFY2027 Fair Share certification.

Year four of the revenue number driving national adoption: $2.2B, then $2.99B, then $3.38B, each more than double the original $1.2B estimate. The revenue is capital-gains-heavy and has only existed in a rising market. Potential impact: another beat extends the proponents’ best argument into the 2028 cycle; a first miss or decline is the first empirical crack, and every state watching will reprice.

2028
Jan. 1, 2028RISurtax phase two: 2%.

The ratchet’s second step, arriving during the 2028 session in which the broader Fair Share package (including the 1% financial-assets tax) will be pressed again under the governor elected in November 2026.

Jan. 1, 2028WAThe 9.9% tax takes effect, if it survived.

Assuming the tax survived both the Supreme Court and the I-645 repeal vote, it attaches to income earned from this date, with first returns and payments in 2029. Withholding, estimated-payment behavior, and any pre-effective-date relocations become the first hard data on a new-state income tax at this rate.

Nov. 7, 2028ILNext constitutional-amendment election.

Illinois’s flat-tax clause (Art. IX, Sec. 3) forecloses any graduated surtax by statute; HJRCA 21 (a 3% surtax over $1M) missed the 2026 ballot deadline. Nov. 2028 is the next opportunity, and the 2020 graduated-tax amendment’s 53-47 defeat is the base rate. Potential impact: passage would unlock the largest currently-barred state; a second defeat likely closes the question for a decade.

Nov. 7, 2028ORNext initiative election.

IP 70 (a 2% annual wealth tax over $30M) failed to qualify in 2026, with press accounts of its filings reporting about $2,325 raised against a 117,173-signature requirement. Oregon’s open initiative process (simple majority) keeps the door structurally open; whether 2028 brings a funded campaign depends heavily on the CA Prop 40 result and the WA court outcome.

Methodology

▶How each view is built and verified

Rates. All 51 top marginal rates are verified against primary and authoritative sources current to August 11, 2026: the Tax Foundation’s 2026 rate table as the base, with every post-publication enactment layered in from primary sources, including Georgia HB 463 (4.99%, retroactive to Jan 1), Arkansas Acts 1 and 2 (3.7%, May 2026), South Carolina H.4216 (5.21% restructure), North Carolina S.L. 2026-41 (signed July 7, 2026, replacing the trigger-dependent schedule with date-certain cuts from 2027; the 2026 rate remains 3.99%), Maryland’s 2025 millionaire brackets, the Maine and Rhode Island surtaxes, Washington SB 6346, and Hawaii’s Act 24, which enacts a 13% bracket on income over $1M effective tax year 2027 (the 2026 top rate remains 11%). The Massachusetts surtax threshold reflects the indexed 2026 figure of $1,107,750. Where the compilation and primary law diverge, primary law controls: Washington’s capital gains top tier is 9.9% (7% plus a 2.9% surcharge over $1M) per the Department of Revenue’s special notice, correcting the 9% figure carried in the Tax Foundation table.

Risk model. The short-term outlook scores each jurisdiction’s likelihood of new enactment or material expansion of a high-income or wealth tax beyond current enacted law within a 24-month window. Enacted law with a future effective date is certainty, not risk: those changes are recorded as slated changes in each state’s Current Status panel and are excluded from the score. Five weighted factors: live legislative vehicles, political composition, activist infrastructure, budget pressure, and contagion, with constitutional barriers applied as a cap rather than a weight. Tiers map to probability bands (Very High above 70%, High 40–70%, Moderate 15–40%, Low 5–15%, Very Low below 5%). Ratings move only on documented events, and each call is timestamped so accuracy can be scored over time. These are analytical judgments, not verified facts.

Active proposals. The second view classifies each jurisdiction by the posture of its live vehicles rather than by whether a proposal exists on paper. Certified for the 2026 ballot applies to California alone. Active vehicle covers a filed bill or budget provision that is live or whose sponsors have signaled a return (New York, Vermont, Illinois, Maryland, Virginia, and Rhode Island) plus Colorado, where Initiative 195, a citizen initiative for graduated rates of 3.7% to 8.4%, submitted signatures on Aug. 3, 2026 that are pending verification. Exploratory, dormant, or failed this cycle covers Massachusetts (union exploration with no filing), Hawaii (a wealth tax refiled across sessions without advancing), and Oregon (IP 70, which failed to qualify in July 2026). The remaining forty jurisdictions have nothing tracked. The distinction matters because a measure that has died for the cycle is not a live threat.

Ballot access. The third view maps whether citizens can put tax measures on the ballot directly, bypassing the legislature. Twelve states allow direct initiative with simple-majority approval; six allow initiated statutes only; four maintain supermajority or legislative gates (including Florida’s two-thirds rule for new taxes and Arizona’s Prop 132); twenty-nine jurisdictions have no workable pathway for tax measures. Utah, North Dakota, and South Dakota vote in November 2026 on measures that would raise their own initiative thresholds; Missouri voters rejected Amendment 4, its version of the change, on August 4, 2026; and Florida and Arkansas enacted process restrictions in 2025.

Events. The fifth view is a dated watch list of twenty-six events through 2028: compliance deadlines, elections, court rulings, effective dates, and filing windows, in chronological order with the anchoring background for each. Dates are verified to the sources in the register; items with no set date carry the best documented window. Impact characterizations are model judgments. Resolved items will be marked, not removed.

The mid-2026 state of play. Rhode Island’s phased surtax was signed June 12 alongside a second-home surcharge effective July 1; New York’s pied-à-terre tax took effect July 1 with implementation rules proposed; California’s Proposition 40 qualified June 17 and survived the withdrawal deadline; Hawaii’s 13% bracket arrives in 2027; Washington’s Initiative 645, repealing the 9.9% tax, was certified for the November ballot on July 15. Not everything advances: Massachusetts’ income tax cut initiative was removed by the SJC on June 18 (its Chapter 62F tax-cap companion remains on the November ballot), and Oregon’s IP 70 wealth tax failed to qualify. Massachusetts reported final FY2026 Fair Share collections of $3.38 billion, up 13%, more than twice the original estimate for a third straight year. On the cut side, North Carolina’s budget (S.L. 2026-41, signed July 7) locked in date-certain reductions to 3.49% beginning in 2027 and referred a 3.5% constitutional income tax cap to the November ballot, and on August 4 Missouri voters rejected Amendment 4, which would have raised the bar for citizen-initiated constitutional amendments, 80.3% to 19.7%. Early August added three fronts: a Richmond County justice halted the pied-à-terre rollout with a temporary restraining order on Aug. 10 (stayed on the city’s appeal, hearing Aug. 31); Washington’s fight over the I-645 ballot disclosure reached the state Supreme Court on Aug. 10; and in Colorado, Initiative 195, a graduated-rate income tax measure, submitted signatures on Aug. 3, now pending verification.

Risk Model (draft modeling)

▶How the short-term outlook is scored, and what the numbers are not
Draft modeling disclaimer. The scoring framework below is draft modeling under active development. Factor weights, bands, and ceilings reflect calibrated analytical judgment, not statistically validated parameters, and both the rubric and individual scores are expected to be revised as the model is reviewed and as its timestamped calls begin to resolve. Nothing in this section is a prediction of certainty, and no rating should be relied on as one.
Each jurisdiction receives a composite score from five weighted factors. Structural obstacles are then applied as a ceiling rather than a deduction, because momentum cannot compress a multi-year constitutional path into a two-year window. The score determines the tier; the tier implies a probability band.

Structural ceiling K. A binding obstacle caps the published tier no matter how high the composite runs. The ceiling is what makes Illinois land at Low on a composite of 58: the flat-tax clause forecloses a statutory surtax, and the next amendment ballot is November 2028, outside this horizon.

What the numbers are and are not. The bands are calibrated judgment, not estimates fitted to historical frequencies, and there is no claim that a composite of 60 has been observed to convert 40 to 70 percent of the time. The rubric exists so that ratings are reproducible, so that changes can be attributed to a specific factor moving, and so that the record can be graded. Every rating is timestamped; resolved calls are scored at the close of the window or when the underlying event resolves.
